Haydn, not Mahler

Is the good news about compliance good news about cooperation? Why near-perfect treaty compliance can be a symptom of shallow cooperation.

George W. Downs, David M. Rocke & Peter N. Barsoom · International Organization 50 (3): 379–406
Scroll to raise the curtain ↓
Overture

A parable about school orchestras

Before any treaties, Downs, Rocke and Barsoom tell a story about a school board, two orchestras and a budget cut. The rest of the article builds on it.

The curtain rises A longtime music teacher publishes an article arguing that funding cutbacks are wrecking school music programs.

The skeptic A school board member who has pushed hard to cut “frivolous” spending reads it and suspects a self-interested plea. So she decides to go and measure for herself.

The research design She attends fifteen concerts in her own district, which cut its music budget, and fifteen in a rival district that kept full funding.

The measurement She counts the mistakes. The two orchestras make about the same number, and very few of them.

The treatment Her district managed this despite cutting the number of rehearsals by 75 percent.

The verdict She is delighted. The cuts did no harm, and rehearsals don’t make orchestras better. They just fund teachers’ “hot tubs and Steinway pianos.”

The catch Look at the music stands. Facing fewer rehearsals, her district’s orchestras chose easier music. No orchestra wants to embarrass itself, so they play Haydn rather than Mahler or Stravinsky.

The lesson The repertoire is a choice, and the orchestra makes it knowing how much it can rehearse. Unless she controls for how hard the music is, counting mistakes tells her nothing about what rehearsals do.

What she saw Here is her evidence: two orchestras, about three mistakes per concert each.

What she missed Spread them out by how hard their music is. They aren’t attempting the same thing at all.

How rehearsal works Rehearsal hardly matters for easy pieces and matters enormously for hard ones. At Haydn’s end the two curves nearly touch, and that is exactly where the under-rehearsed orchestra chose to play.

The missing data The concert that would reveal what rehearsals are worth is one that never takes place: the under-rehearsed orchestra attempting Mahler.

Now swap the words Rehearsals → enforcement. Mistakes → violations. Difficulty of the repertoire → depth of cooperation. Choosing the program → choosing which treaties to sign.

That is the whole article in miniature: a treaty, like a concert program, is an endogenous choice.

I

The good news about compliance

By the mid-1990s a group of political scientists and international lawyers, whom the authors call the managerial school, had reached a cheerful conclusion about international regulatory agreements.

First movement States comply with their agreements most of the time. Not occasionally: across arms control, trade and environmental regimes, compliance is generally quite good.

Second movement And not because they fear punishment. For Abram and Antonia Chayes, compliance comes from an ongoing process of interaction that restores “the balance of advantage” that produced the agreement in the first place.

Third movement When states do fall short, it is rarely deliberate defiance. Noncompliance is an administrative breakdown, a problem to be solved rather than an offense to be punished:

“Persuasion and argument are the principal motors of this process.”

Finale So the way forward is better management. Oran Young describes a new understanding that treats compliance “as a management problem rather than an enforcement problem.”

Program notes The managerial diagnosis has three causes: ambiguous treaties, states with limited capacity, and social or economic change nobody controls. Each has a matching cure, and transparency matters most. A state that expects to be caught gains less from cheating, sanctions or no sanctions.

What gets left out Enforcement, by contrast, is too costly, too political and too coercive. Sanctions mostly work against weak states, only great powers can impose them, and retaliation risks “a long echo of alternating retaliations.”

If this is right, fifty years of realist gloom about anarchy were overdone, and so was game theorists’ fixation on the repeated prisoners’ dilemma.

The authors call the findings “interesting and important,” but argue that the policy lessons drawn from them are “dangerously contaminated by selection problems.” Downs, Rocke & Barsoom, p. 379–80

II

The treaties you never see

States choose their treaties the way orchestras choose their repertoire. Studying only the treaties that exist is like counting mistakes without looking at the music.

The menu States choose their treaties “from an infinitely large set of possible treaties.” Each dot is one agreement they could make, placed by how deep its cuts go.

The price tag Each possible treaty needs some threat of punishment to make cheating unprofitable. Deeper treaties generally need far more (Chapter IV shows why), and some need more than any punishment can supply: the ∞ row.

The dots differ because issue areas differ: some are nearly coordination games with little temptation, others are sharp prisoners’ dilemmas.

The budget Enforcement is costly and politically awkward, so states will only supply so much. Suppose they will credibly threaten an eight-period trade war, and no more.

Selection “States will rarely spend a great deal of time and effort negotiating agreements that will continually be violated.” What gets signed is a pile of shallow treaties.

What the managerialists measured Now measure compliance among the treaties that exist. It is excellent, and the few violations look like honest accidents. Enforcement appears irrelevant.

What the measure hides Had states signed everything on the menu without more enforcement, compliance would collapse. A high compliance rate can mean enforcement doesn’t matter, or it can mean states only make agreements that need little of it. Compliance data alone can’t tell the two apart.

III

What “depth” means

To fix the selection problem, you need the treaty equivalent of musical difficulty. The authors propose the depth of cooperation.

A definition Depth is the extent to which a treaty requires states to depart from what they would have done without it. Picture a collectively harmful behavior, tariffs or pollution, rising for decades until a treaty is signed.

Counterfactual no. 1 What would have happened without the treaty is never observed. One crude estimate is the status quo at the time of signing.

Counterfactual no. 2 Another is to extend the trend: the year-to-year rate of change before signing.

Measuring depth Depth is the gap between the counterfactual and what the treaty requires. The authors measure it at the treaty’s required level, because that is the level compliance is judged against. Here a 20% cut is 20% deep against the status quo and 32% deep against the trend.

A different pollutant Now take a pollutant that is already declining. A 30% cut looks ambitious against the status quo, yet it is almost exactly what the trend predicted anyway.

Perfect compliance, no depth Emissions keep following their trend and the treaty is met in full: 100% compliance, almost zero depth. Chapter V shows this is the pattern the article describes for the Montreal Protocol.

Both estimates are crude, and in some areas the scope for cooperation is genuinely smaller. But the authors see no reason the crudeness should distort the relationship between depth and the enforcement it requires.

IV

Why deeper treaties need sharper teeth

The theoretical core of the article: a repeated trade game between two states, in which the incentive to cheat outruns the value of cooperating.

The game States A and B set tariffs every period. Without a treaty, both protect at the noncooperative level (100%). The authors assume protection pays politically because interest groups reward it. Consider a treaty that cuts both tariffs to 70%.

The temptation Whatever the other side does, each state does better with higher tariffs…

The dilemma …yet both prefer the treaty to the trade war. It is a prisoners’ dilemma: a mixed-motive game, not a coordination game in which nobody wants to cheat.

Depth is a dial Unlike the textbook dilemma, the choices here are continuous. A treaty can be set at any depth, cheating can be “limited or flagrant,” and punishment can last one period or forever. The value of keeping a treaty rises with depth, but more and more slowly.

The article’s Figure 1 Now add the payoff from cheating. If your partner keeps the treaty, your best one-period response is to raise your own tariffs. The temptation to cheat rises much faster than the value of the treaty. Notice how the treaty curve flattens once the axis rescales.

Scroll to deepen As the treaty deepens from 5% to 60%, a single period of cheating becomes worth many periods of cooperation.

The constraint The shaded gap is the one-time gain any punishment must wipe out. In the authors’ words, it is what “imposes a limit on which treaties can be supported.”

Punishment, in periods Here is the deterrent. If B cheats, A abandons the treaty for some number of periods. Future payoffs are discounted at δ = 0.95, a 5% interest rate. A 5% cut backed by a one-period punishment: cheating still pays.

Enough Two periods of punishment make cheating unprofitable. That matches the article’s example: a 5% treaty “only requires a punishment of two periods.”

Deeper treaty, same threat Deepen the treaty to 30% and keep the two-period threat. Cheating now pays handsomely.

A longer shadow Holding a 30% treaty takes a punishment of about 27 periods.

Beyond reach At 40%, even permanent reversion to the trade war (the “grim strategy”) can’t deter cheating. No punishment is long enough.

The article’s Figure 2 Put the pieces together. The punishment needed to hold a treaty grows with its depth, slowly at first and then explosively.

Shallow is cheap Shallow treaties need almost no enforcement, which is exactly the kind of treaty the managerialists studied.

A hard ceiling 37.19% is the deepest treaty that even permanent punishment can support. The essential point: “the deeper the agreement is, the greater the punishments required to support it.”

Patience moves the wall States that care more about the future can sustain deeper treaties with the same threats. At δ = 0.98 the ceiling is about 63%; at δ = 0.90 it falls to about 21%.

Fairness takes a back seat

Notice what the model does not ask of punishment. It need not be tit-for-tat, matching restriction for restriction, and it need not hurt the cheater as much as the cheating hurt the victim. Its only job is to cost the transgressor at least as much as the violation could gain it.

“Fairness and justice must take a back seat to the correct disincentive.” p. 386

Nor does it matter much who punishes. Enforcement can work through linkage, as between the superpowers in the Kissinger years. It can run through formal institutions like GATT’s dispute settlement, through unilateral action such as U.S. enforcement of fishery and wildlife agreements under the Pelly and Packwood-Magnuson amendments, or through domestic law, as in the European Union. Like the managerialists, the authors suspect the most effective schemes are decentralized. What matters is the magnitude of the punishment, not its mechanism.

Back to the orchestra

If the managerialists’ high compliance and scarce enforcement arise because states avoid deep cooperation, being unwilling or unable to pay for enforcement, then their prescription is self-defeating. Telling states to ignore enforcement would be “equivalent to telling the school orchestras to avoid wasting their time rehearsing.” It would confine them to coordination games and shallow prisoners’ dilemmas: Haydn forever.

The authors concede the limit of the argument. If mixed-motive games are rare in real regulation, as the managerialists implicitly assume, “the argument fizzles.” Preferences can’t be observed directly, so instead of cataloguing the underlying games they test two implications:

Test 1 · Depth

If the managerialists are right, there should be many deep agreements with little enforcement. If the game theorists are right, there should be few, or else violations everywhere. Since violations are not rampant, the authors expect the former.

Test 2 · Violations

Selection distorts the set of violations less than the set of treaties. Prisoners’-dilemma treaties should be over-represented among violations, so self-interest should show up in them rather than ambiguity or incapacity alone.

V

Test 1: the rarity of deep cooperation

Where does real-world cooperation sit, by depth and by enforcement?

The two predictions Arrange agreements by depth and enforcement. The managerial view predicts plenty of agreements in the lower-right corner: deep but barely enforced. The enforcement view predicts that corner is nearly empty.

Arms control, part 1 Start with U.S. arms agreements since 1945. Some, like the “Hot Line,” don’t regulate weapons at all. The Antarctic, Outer Space and Seabed treaties kept states on the path they were already on: neither superpower had plans for those weapons. No wonder there has been no reported deviation in four decades. Tacit threats of retaliation were present anyway.

Arms control, part 2 SALT I froze ICBM launchers at the status quo, while the United States had none under construction and the Soviet Union could finish those it was building. SALT II was hardly deeper.

How shallow? SALT II let MIRVed ICBMs grow by 40%, and total nuclear weapons by 50 to 70%. Jozef Goldblat noted a “remarkable compatibility” between the treaty limits and both sides’ planned programs.

A treaty that deepened by itself In 1972 neither side had the technology or budget for a major ABM system. As technology advanced, the treaty grew deeper against what the U.S. would otherwise build, while enforcement weakened with the Soviet Union. Game theory predicts pressure on the treaty from the stronger party. “This appears to have occurred.”

Genuinely deep? INF, CFE and START are deeper. Are they deep cooperation without enforcement? The authors say there is “no easy answer”: the counterfactual describes a political system that no longer exists. Nobody measures NATO’s depth by comparing Germany’s wartime and postwar behavior.

Real cuts START cut delivery vehicles by about 30% and warheads by 40%, and INF eliminated a whole class of missiles. These are significant against both the status quo and the trend. They remain hard to read, because the Cold War rivalry that defined the counterfactual had ended.

The environment Environmental agreements look much the same. The Montreal Protocol’s apparently large CFC cuts codified reductions “polluters were voluntarily prepared to accomplish” (Murdoch and Sandler), and most of the cuts came before ratification.

The cleanest test The best test is a policy area whose rules tightened over time. In trade and European integration, depth and enforcement rose together.

Trade, before Under GATT, panels moved at a ponderous pace, either party could veto a ruling, and sanctions were used exactly once.

Trade, after The Uruguay Round “substantially reduced” major trade barriers (Bayard and Elliott), and it arrived with an enforcement system that is more automatic and harder to manipulate. In Europe, the deepening at Maastricht came as member states chose to strengthen the European Court’s power “to monitor and punish defections.”

VI

Test 2: why states really break the rules

Selection distorts the set of violations less than the set of treaties. If the managerialists are right, violations should come from ambiguity, incapacity and bad luck, and better management should fix them.

Case 1: the Washington Naval Treaty Arms control scholars agree it was the most ambitious agreement of its kind, and the most violated. Here are warships at their announced or permitted sizes…

As built …and as built. Japan “broke the rules seriously, systematically, and often clandestinely” (Kaufman). The United States and Italy cheated too.

Diagnosis Ambiguity explains some disputes, because terms like “naval base” and “fortification” were left undefined. It doesn’t explain systematic cheating on tonnage, and incapacity or uncontrollable change explain even less. The U.S. failed to detect most of Japan’s violations, and domestic politics stopped it responding to those it did detect.

A pattern across cases Look across the article’s cases. Failures cluster where violations are hard to see and cheap to commit. Fisheries commissions faltered because no state saw much reason to restrain its own fishermen while others flouted the rules. The Mediterranean Plan reached consensus by dropping meaningful restrictions and enforcement, and the result was a collapsing ecosystem.

What worked Successes pair visibility with sanctions. The 1995 North Atlantic fisheries accord was hailed as a model because of “stiff fines and other penalties.” GATT’s violations were mostly concessions to protectionist interest groups: EC oilseed subsidies, U.S. sugar quotas, Japanese beef and citrus limits. U.S. retaliation, or the threat of it, eventually helped produce the WTO’s tougher procedures.

After detection, what? Fred Iklé’s classic question sums it up. Transparency matters because of the reaction it provokes, and that reaction is enforcement.

Case 2: the managerialists’ own showcase Ronald Mitchell’s study of oil pollution from tankers is held up as an archetype of the managerial approach. From 1954 to 1978, rules limited how much oil tankers could discharge at sea. Discharges ran three to thirty times the legal limit.

A new strategy In 1978 the regime switched to regulating equipment: tankers had to install segregated ballast tanks. It was costly and cut cargo space, yet owners installed them.

Why it worked Equipment is easy to inspect, and the penalty had teeth: ships without the certificate could be barred or detained in port, at roughly $20,000 a day in lost business, far more than typical fines. Transparency was critical, but so was a sanction that hurt.

VII

Enforcement and the future of cooperation

Early agreements may need little enforcement. Continued progress means facing treaties where defection pays.

Changing the game Deeper cooperation can come without more enforcement if the game itself changes so that cheating pays less. The authors grant that technology, relative prices, domestic transitions and ideas have probably done more for cooperation than “all efforts at dispute resolution and enforcement combined.”

A friendlier game Halve the temptation, and the same maximal punishment holds a far deeper treaty.

It cuts both ways But ideas and prices aren’t levers policymakers know how to pull, and they can move the wrong way. The nonproliferation regime “has shown signs of fraying” as nuclear weapons became relatively cheaper.

Why not just enforce harder? There is an agency problem. A leader gains from giving in to protectionists whenever interest groups pay a premium larger than the electoral cost, for instance during recessions or after a foreign technological breakthrough.

Too soft Set the penalty for violations low and defection is constant.

Too hard to promise Set it very high and leaders still suspect, probably correctly, that the protectionist premium will sometimes exceed any credible punishment.

The bargain leaders strike So they choose penalties high enough to prevent constant defection but low enough to allow it when circumstances demand. Their hand is stayed not by faith that defection is accidental but by knowing “just how unaccidental it is.”

Broader or deeper? One way out is to restrict membership to states that won’t need to defect often, so those inside can set and enforce a deeper standard. With all twelve of these toy states admitted, the deepest treaty everyone will keep is shallow.

A small club Admit only the five least tempted, and the treaty can go much deeper.

An open question In this toy, total gains peak in between. That may be why deeply cooperative regimes tend to have few members and large regimes shallow cooperation. The authors leave it as a question, and took it up two years later in “Managing the Evolution of Multilateralism” (IO, 1998).

Coda The managerial findings are real: compliance is high, and transparency, assistance and dispute resolution do matter. But high compliance with shallow treaties says little about what deep treaties will require. To dismiss enforcement on the strength of high compliance rates, the authors conclude, is “dangerously premature.”

The argument in six bars

  1. The good news. Treaty compliance is high, and enforcement is rare.
  2. The catch. Treaties are chosen. States rarely negotiate agreements that would be violated constantly.
  3. The measure. Depth of cooperation is how far a treaty moves states from what they would have done anyway.
  4. The mechanism. In mixed-motive games the gain from cheating grows faster than the value of a treaty, so deeper treaties need bigger punishments. Some depths can’t be held at all.
  5. The evidence. Deep cooperation without enforcement is rare. Where cooperation deepened, as in GATT to WTO and the EC to Maastricht, enforcement deepened with it. Violations look self-interested, and transparency works when sanctions back it.
  6. The stakes. Prescribing management instead of enforcement condemns states to Haydn.

Questions for discussion

  1. The depth measure relies on a counterfactual (status quo or trend). Which treaties in your own research area would look deep on one and shallow on the other?
  2. Could managerialists turn the selection argument around? Could high compliance reflect states learning to write better treaties rather than avoiding hard ones?
  3. The model treats punishment as reverting to the trade war, which also hurts the punisher. How does the credibility of punishment change the ceiling?
  4. The authors predict that self-interested violations are over-represented among violations. What sampling design would actually test this?
  5. Is the broader-versus-deeper trade-off visible in today’s climate regime, from Kyoto’s targets to Paris’s pledge-and-review?
  6. If “ideas and relative prices” do most of the work, what does that imply about where political scientists should look for leverage?
